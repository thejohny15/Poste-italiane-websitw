US vs OECD Europe Consumption (Petroleum & Other Liquids, Monthly)

Loading monthly US vs OECD Europe consumption...

This graph suggests that, in the event of a prolonged disruption linked to the war in Iran, the United States is less vulnerable than OECD Europe to an actual shortage of petroleum supply. U.S. production has been close to, and at times above, U.S. consumption, whereas OECD Europe consumes far more oil than it produces. However, this does not mean that the United States would be insulated from the conflict. Oil is priced on an integrated world market, and recent EIA forecasts already show Brent jumping sharply after the onset of military action in the Middle East, with higher U.S. gasoline and diesel prices expected as a result. This matters especially because the United States remains the world’s largest oil consumer at roughly 20 million barrels per day, while China, the second-largest, consumed about 16 million barrels per day in 2024. A price shock therefore affects a very large base of U.S. demand. In addition, the U.S. economy is structurally sensitive to energy prices: around two-thirds of U.S. petroleum is consumed in transportation, petroleum products still account for about 89% of transportation energy use, and private cars dominate mobility far more than in Europe, where passenger cars account for roughly 72% of passenger transport. Finally, even beyond transport fuels, higher energy prices can spread through the wider U.S. economy because electricity demand is rising, while natural gas still provides more than 40% of U.S. electricity generation.

US & Euro Area Inflation (YoY) vs Naphtha Price (Monthly)

Loading monthly inflation + naphtha price...

Naphtha matters for inflation because it is a key input into petrochemicals, especially plastics. That means a sustained rise in naphtha prices can raise costs far beyond the energy sector itself, through packaging, consumer goods and industrial materials. The effect is less visible than gasoline, but it is potentially very broad. In that sense, the Iran war is not only an oil shock; it is also a supply-chain shock for petrochemicals. Reports already indicate that Asian refiners and petrochemical producers are cutting runs and, in some cases, shutting units because of disrupted Middle Eastern feedstock supply. If this persists for months rather than weeks, the inflation impact could become more generalized.
